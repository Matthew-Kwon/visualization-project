Where did complaints against NYPD officers come from, and how many were upheld?

This map explores which NYC precincts received the most civilian complaints against NYPD officers from 2000 to 2020, who filed them, and how often they were substantiated. Move the year slider, filter by complainant or allegation type, and click a precinct to see its details.

Write-up

Motivation

In my initial analysis of the data, I figured out that the complaints soared since 2000 and they are mostly from a few precincts such as 75. I wanted to see if complaints against NYPD officers are spread evenly across the city or stick to a few neighborhoods, and whether that changed over time. A single static map hides the year-to-year change and also hides who was filing, so I made the map interactive.

Design rationale

  • Encoding. Precincts are areas, so I used a choropleth with a sequential yellow-orange-red palette (light = few, dark = many). Counts are not negative and have no meaningful middle, so a diverging palette didn't fit. Precincts with no complaints are light grey so "zero" doesn't look like "low".
  • Fixed color domain. The max of the scale is the largest single-year value over 2000-2020 for the current filters. If I rescaled every year, moving the slider would make every year look equally dark.
  • Count vs. rate. Raw counts mostly follow how busy a precinct is, so I added a substantiated % view not to mislead the viewer. (There wasn't any data of total population, so I couldn't visualize directly with % of complaints per precinct) For the rate I hide precincts with fewer than 5 complaints, because 1 out of 1 = 100% is misleading. The color scale with 5 complaints still can mislead the viewer to exaggerate the proportion of substantiated complaints but much less than without the hiding.
  • Interactions. The year slider shows change over time. The ethnicity and allegation filters let you ask questions like "where do Force complaints from Hispanic complainants happen?". Clicking a precinct opens small bar charts (ethnicity, gender, age, outcome) linked to the map, so you can compare one precinct with the city.
  • Alternatives I considered. A line chart per precinct (77 lines was unreadable), a bubble map (circles overlapped in small precincts), and normalizing by population (I didn't have clean precinct-level population data, so I left it out instead of guessing).
  • Data transformations. The raw file is in the form of csv which is not ideal for visualizing interative map. I converted it to JSON format to make it easier to visualize. The raw file has one row per allegation, so I deduplicated by complaint_id and counted each unique complaint once. I kept complaints received 2000-2020, placed each one in the precinct where the incident happened, and dropped precincts that are not on the map (0, 1000, or missing). Missing/refused ethnicity is grouped as "Unknown". A complaint with several complainants or allegation types counts once in each matching filter, but only once under "All". Substantiated rate is the share of complaints where the CCRB substantiated at least one allegation.
  • Data source. Civilian Complaints Against New York City Police Officers

Development process

I worked on this alone. It took about 10 hours in total. The most time went into the data: figuring out that the CSV has one row per allegation and not per complaint, so my first counts were too high. Turning the CSV to JSON and deduplicating the data also took a while. Getting the precinct GeoJSON to draw correctly in D3 also took a while.
